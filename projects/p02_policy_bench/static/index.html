Discrimination, and then the cutoff

These are two different questions and they are kept apart. How well the score separates borrowers who repay from borrowers who do not is a property of the scorecard, and moving the cutoff cannot change it. What the business gives up at each cutoff is a property of the decision.

What happens as the book ages and the applicants change

Four questions that need a cohort with dates, and that every other tab on this page cannot ask. The borrowers arrive over a twenty-four month window and the population drifts while the policy stands still — which is the situation a scorecard is actually used in.

Vintage curves — default rate by months on book

One line per origination quarter. Read down a column, never across a row: the youngest vintage has had the least time to go bad, so its headline rate is lower for a reason that has nothing to do with the policy. Four of the eight quarters are drawn; the table has all of them.

Population stability — the score, and then its inputs

PSI compares the early window with the later one. Under 0.10 is conventionally “stable”, 0.10–0.25 worth looking at, over 0.25 a population that has moved. The conventional reading is a convention, not a theorem, and it is quoted as one.

Out of time — what the cutoff promised, and what it earned

The cutoff is chosen on the early months and then applied to the later ones. The two windows hold different numbers of applicants, so the totals are not comparable and the per-loan figures are.

Only the approved have an outcome

A declined applicant never borrows, so never repays or defaults. This cohort is generated and therefore knows what they would have done; a real book does not, and the gap between these two numbers is the size of the bias that reject inference argues about.
